# RUL backtest

Generated by `python -m ml.train_rul <subset>`. Regenerate everything with:

```bash
python scripts/fetch_prognostics_data.py
for s in FD001 FD002 FD003 FD004; do python -m ml.train_rul $s; done
```

## What is being measured

NASA C-MAPSS turbofan degradation data. Each engine runs from healthy to failure. The
training engines are observed all the way to failure; the benchmark's test engines are
cut off partway through, and a separate file holds the true remaining cycles at the cut.
Those labels are ground truth and are never visible during fitting.

Every split is by engine. A unit's cycles never straddle a boundary, so the model is
never scored on an engine it has already seen part of.

The target is clipped at 125 cycles. Degradation is not observable on a nearly new
engine, so the model is not asked to tell 300 cycles of life from 250.

## Three data sets, and why there are three

| set | built from | purpose |
|---|---|---|
| fit | 60% of training engines | fitting the models |
| calibration | 20% of training engines, truncated at random points | setting the conformal offset |
| internal test | 20% of training engines, truncated the **same** way | coverage where the guarantee applies |
| official test | the benchmark's own test engines | external check under known shift |

The calibration and internal test sets are produced by an identical random truncation
process, which is what makes them exchangeable and the conformal guarantee meaningful.
The official test set is not exchangeable with them: the benchmark truncates its engines
toward later life, so only 11% of its points sit at the RUL cap against 39% of training
cycles. That shift is real and its cost is reported rather than hidden. See F-08.

## Point estimate

| subset | fit / cal / internal engines | official test engines | RMSE | MAE | baseline RMSE |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| FD001 | 60 / 20 / 20  | 100 | 17.00 | 12.19 | 41.81 |
| FD002 | 156 / 52 / 52 | 259 | 17.57 | 12.60 | 44.92 |
| FD003 | 60 / 20 / 20  | 100 | 16.70 | 12.53 | 43.71 |
| FD004 | 149 / 50 / 50 | 248 | 19.24 | 13.48 | 45.30 |

Errors are in cycles, measured on the official test engines. The baseline is the RMSE of
predicting the training mean for every engine, which is what any model has to beat before
it is worth anything. The model beats it by roughly 2.4x to 2.6x throughout.

`test_phm08` in the JSON files is the benchmark's own asymmetric penalty, which punishes
predicting failure later than it happens harder than predicting it early, because a late
warning means the machine already broke.

## Lower bound

The model reports **"at least N cycles remain"**, not an interval. For maintenance
planning that is the number a decision rests on, and an upper bound is not identifiable
here anyway: with 39% of the target sitting at the cap, a fitted upper quantile returns
the cap for every engine and can never be wrong. Reporting that as calibration would have
been a band that cannot fail. See F-08.

Target coverage is 0.90.

| subset | calibration engines | internal coverage | official coverage | conformal offset (cycles) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| FD001 | 20 | 0.820 | 0.800 | 1.79 |
| FD002 | 52 | 0.985 | 0.950 | 5.76 |
| FD003 | 20 | 0.890 | 0.770 | 2.60 |
| FD004 | 50 | 0.900 | 0.915 | 5.60 |

**How to read this.** Coverage on the calibration set itself is exactly 0.905 by
construction, so the conformal arithmetic is correct. On fresh exchangeable engines it
scatters from 0.820 to 0.985 around the 0.90 target. The bound is not biased, it is
noisy, and the reason is that cycles within one engine are strongly correlated: on FD001
the median per-engine coverage is 0.90, but three of twenty engines score under 0.5 and
one at 0.20, each dragging ten correlated points with it.

The effective sample size is the number of engines, not the number of rows. The two
subsets with fifty-plus calibration engines sit nearer the target than the two with
twenty.

Official coverage sits below internal coverage on three of four subsets, which is the
measured price of the distribution shift described above.

None of these numbers were tuned to look better. Raising the quantile until test coverage
reads 0.90 would fit the test set, which is the one thing this exercise exists to avoid.

## What this does not show

- **The model is fitted to turbofan engines, not CNC spindle bearings.** It shows the
  method works on real degradation data with real held-out labels. It says nothing about
  the demo asset, whose predictions stay rule-based and are labelled
  `declared_thresholds` in every tool result. See decision 008.
- **C-MAPSS is itself produced by a NASA engine simulator.** It is real in the sense that
  matters here: the labels were not written by the person being graded, and the units
  were not chosen after seeing the results. It is not field data.
- **Nothing retrains and nothing watches for drift.** These numbers are a snapshot with a
  date on them, not a promise about next month.
